# The Biology of Collapse

The cycle of growth, collapse and self-destruction appears to be a built-in quality of our culture, society, and even our brain's architecture.  

In order to protect itself, society has always chosen to skip the chapter of collapse altogether when writing its history books - or even better, present it as a "freak" event: a special situation that only ever happens once, like the Holocaust. 

Unless we recognize the entrenched and systemic nature of collapse which makes it so ubiquitous and inevitable, we won't see the end game that is currently playing out.  

Species that use their intellect for predation can become aberrant, invasive entities within the ecosystem.  Such species are fundamentally incapable of coexisting with others, because their intelligence has become a weapon of mass destruction able to overcome all ecosystem limits: resource usage, territory, and population thresholds.  

But intelligence has yet another enemy to contend with: itself. Humans are cancerous entities not only to Earth, but to each other.   

The ecosystem always strikes back, because unlike ever-expanding human empires, it seeks balance, not excess.

The job of a mycologist may sound geeky and uninteresting, but we get to witness the rise and fall of civilisations within a matter of days: each fungal culture in a Petri dish samicrography of humanity.  

Exponential growth at breakneck speed is the defining quality of early-stage capitalism: population spills out into the country like a virus, luxury becomes the norm, and innovations turn obsolete before they even enter the market.  

As the fungal colony begins to approach the edges of the petri dish, growth has already slowed down significantly. The availability of food is not what it used to be, and competition is rife. But what the fungal colony has lost in momentum, it has gained in size, experience, and extractive capacity. It is now a formidable, massive civilisation of billions of cells. Population size is the defining quality at this stage, and it is not unusual to see mutations emerge, leading to he specialisation of individuals and formation of complex societies: fungal cells with unique adaptations help the colony continue by creating new capabilities: some of these cells make new, harsh chemicals and enzymes that can break down some of the nutrients the colony wasn't able to previously utilize

As the civilisation grows, these mutants become increasingly import- ant and eventually take control of the colony. Despite having all but exhausted their resources, civilisations continue to grow by transforming themselves into efficient, exploitative societies. This is the threshold where the civilisation crosses into late- stage capitalism.
