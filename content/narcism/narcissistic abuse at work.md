# daydreaming

https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8751151/

According to a growing body of literature, maladaptive daydreaming (MD) is characterized as “extensive imagination activity that replaces human interaction and/or interferes with academic, interpersonal, or vocational functioning,” [[1](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8751151/#B1-ijerph-19-00386)]. Somer’s seminal work on six maladaptive daydreamers (MDers) [[1](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8751151/#B1-ijerph-19-00386)] featured a description of captivity, rescue and escape, and idealized self as central MD motifs. MDers can lose themselves for hours in vivid, highly structured dreams, frequently with a strong sense of being present in the daydream.

MD is a subset of the greater cognitive phenomena of daydreaming (i.e., a waking fantasy activity), which is a common, healthy mental activity that 96 percent of Americans engage in [[2](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8751151/#B2-ijerph-19-00386)]. According to Killingsworth and Gilbert (2010) [[3](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8751151/#B3-ijerph-19-00386)], this brain process accounts for over half of all human thought, and the average person appears to have hundreds of daydreaming episodes per day [[4](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8751151/#B4-ijerph-19-00386)].

Daydreaming may operate on a continuum from healthy daydreaming to MD, which has been conceptualized as a psychological dysfunction attendant to trauma [[1](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8751151/#B1-ijerph-19-00386)]. Historically, Freud believed that the function of the daydream was the same as that of night dreams—that is, wish-fulfilment. Freud (1908) [[5](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8751151/#B5-ijerph-19-00386)] wrote, “it was no longer difficult to recognize that night-dreams are wish-fulfilments in just the same way as day-dreams—the phantasies which we all know so well” (p. 149).

Most contemporary psychoanalysts would agree that beyond wish fulfilment, adaptive daydreaming can serve a host of purposes, such as to reverse narcissistic injuries, spur creativity and artistic pursuits, or heighten self-esteem and pleasurable affects. When daydreaming is not determined by defensive functions, it can be flexible and contain elements of reversible dissociation and regression. Along these lines, Kris (1952) [[6](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8751151/#B6-ijerph-19-00386)] famously described how certain regressions can be in the service of the ego, in contrast to other regressions which evidence an ego that is overwhelmed.

“We have now to elucidate in greater detail the relations of wit and caricature to dreams: in dreams, the ego abandons its supremacy, and the primary process obtains control, whereas in wit and caricature the process remains in the service of the ego” (p. 177). This phrase alone suffices to indicate that the problem at hand is a more general one. The distinction between an ego overwhelmed by regression and ‘regression in the service of the ego’ covers a large and imposing range of mental experience.

Other authors have noted how daydreams can serve as a form of motoric self-control, self-restraint, and delay of gratification [[7](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8751151/#B7-ijerph-19-00386),[8](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8751151/#B8-ijerph-19-00386),[9](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8751151/#B9-ijerph-19-00386)]. In its maladaptive form, daydreaming can be rigid and compulsory, leading to self and object fragmentation and experiences of dissociation marked by distressing self-states [[10](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8751151/#B10-ijerph-19-00386)]. MD may be determined by defensive needs and may represent an escape into fantasy and avoidance. Winnicott believed that excessive daydreaming and fantasizing interferes with action and negatively affects dreaming [[11](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8751151/#B11-ijerph-19-00386)].

# What are the main causes of apathy

The main causes of apathy include:

1. Neurological conditions such as Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, Huntington's disease, frontotemporal dementia, and stroke[1][2][3].

2. Psychiatric disorders like major depression, schizophrenia, and PTSD[1][2][3][4].

3. Damage to specific brain regions, particularly the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex and ventral striatum, which are crucial for processing rewards and motivating behavior[2][3].

4. Chronic stress, trauma, or challenging life circumstances that lead to emotional detachment as a coping mechanism[1][4].

5. Burnout and emotional exhaustion, often seen in caregivers or those experiencing prolonged stress[2].

6. Adolescence, where apathy may emerge as individuals navigate identity changes, societal pressures, and academic demands[1].

7. Certain medications, particularly antidepressants, can sometimes cause apathy as a side effect[1].

8. Severe anxiety can lead to emotional fatigue, low serotonin levels, negative thinking, and difficulty enjoying activities, all of which can contribute to apathy[5].
